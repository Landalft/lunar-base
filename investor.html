The risks

Six things that can break it.

Landing

5 of 13 landings since 2019 ended upright and working. The plan starts at 40%.

The price is untested

$30M a survey is our placeholder. One written reply from 11 contacts; nobody has named a price. NASA has paid $10M for one lander's data.

Astrolab has not been asked

Its known deals are payload contracts, not shared ventures. It already has a partner that models the ground.

Open data

NASA asks for unlimited rights to data it pays for. An agency client may want the survey public; resale then shrinks.

One mission, part of a site

A base site is 5–25 km². The first mission has one lunar day: about 1–4 km².

Dates slip

The plan launches in 2029. Space dates move, and a slip costs time before it costs money.

Five forces on the Moon market today

A tough market today, which is why SLB should share the ride and own the measurement.

Rights and law

Nobody can own the site. The data follows the contract.

The ground

Nobody can own it. The Outer Space Treaty of 1967 bars any claim of sovereignty.

What is dug up

United States law, since 2015, lets a company own what it extracts. Luxembourg, the UAE, Japan and Italy have similar laws.

The data

No treaty covers it, so ownership follows the contract. Data paid for by an agency is often made public.

A working site

A “safety zone” protects an operation only while it runs. The Artemis Accords, signed by 76 countries, are not binding.

Who licenses us

Our home country, not the launch country. Our choice is France's 2008 space law, the only binding licence on offer.

The neutron source

Export-controlled, so we classify it early and reuse SLB's existing licences. For launch safety it sits in the lightest US tier.

Ethics

We measure before anyone digs.

Five risks from our register, and what we commit to against each.

Spoiling the ice

We measure before anyone disturbs it, and publish an exhaust budget for every landing.

Greenwashing

Every launch tonne is counted in SLB's net-zero inventory, and the capital is reported separately.

Military use

Civil and peaceful use only, written into every data licence.

Locking up sites

We claim no land and take no exclusive sites. We share training, samples and instrument time with newer space nations.

Closing the data

The basic map is open on day one. Client surveys are released after three to five years.

All ten commitments
  1. Measure every ice site before anyone disturbs it, and publish the baseline openly.
  2. Publish an exhaust-contamination budget for each landing, and keep a set distance from cold traps.
  3. Keep a full inventory of organic materials on polar missions.
  4. Respect heritage sites under NASA's 2011 guidelines, with or without a NASA contract.
  5. Open the coarse baseline data at once; release client surveys after 3–5 years.
  6. Assess the life-cycle footprint of every mission and count it in SLB's net-zero accounts.
  7. Prefer methane or hydrogen launchers, sign the Zero Debris Charter, and plan the disposal of every asset.
  8. Have the benefits on Earth independently checked: leaks found and fixed, carbon stored.
  9. Help write a public standard for reporting lunar resources, with areas set aside for science.
  10. Consult affected communities and share the benefits through science and training.

Assumes: these are commitments the team proposes, not SLB policy.
